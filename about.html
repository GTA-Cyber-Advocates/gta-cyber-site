About

A community of practice, not a silo.

GTA Cyber exists because cybersecurity is a craft practised across every part of Global Tech Advocates, and crafts are better served by communities than by departments.

The parent

Where we sit

Global Tech Advocates is the international grassroots tech community: more than 40,000 business leaders, experts and investors volunteering their time across 42 or more networks worldwide.

It is proudly independent, not-for-profit and volunteer-led. It works because it is designed for busy people on the frontline of the tech sector, and because the norm is to give first.

GTA Cyber is a community of practice within that structure, alongside the resource hubs GTA already runs on subjects such as net zero and disability. It is managed by Vivat Group on behalf of the wider organisation.

The model

Borrowed from engineering, not from org charts

The idea is long established in large organisations: a community of practice keeps specialist craft alive without breaking delivery teams apart. Spotify popularised its own version of this, and the pattern maps unusually well onto a volunteer network.

Vertical

What we avoided

A standalone cyber chapter would have competed with the networks its members already belong to, asked people to pick a side, and cut security off from the rest of GTA at exactly the point where it is most useful.

Horizontal

What we built instead

Members stay in their existing GTA network and keep their day jobs. The community sits across those networks, so a question raised in one city can be answered by a practitioner in another.

Craft

Why it holds together

People join a community of practice because of what they do, not where they are. That shared craft is a stronger bond than geography, and it survives members changing employer, sector or country.

How it runs

Principles we intend to keep

  • Give first. The GTA norm, and the reason the network works. The community asks for time and expertise before it asks for anything else.
  • No selling into the membership. Sponsors fund the programme. They do not get a list, a lead-gen pipeline, or a speaking slot dressed up as a session.
  • Vendor-neutral by default. Guidance published by the community names categories and practices rather than products, including our sponsors’ products.
  • Practitioners set the agenda. What the community works on is decided by the people doing the work, not by whoever is funding it that year.
  • Light on process. Busy people on the frontline is the design constraint. If something needs a committee to happen, it probably should not happen.
